الأَمْثَالُ العَرَبِيَّة

Arabic Proverbs

Objectives — أهداف الدرس

  • Know the vocabulary behind three classical Arabic proverbs — from مَثَلٌ to نَكْبَةٌ.
  • Meet المُثَنَّى (the dual) in a real word — خُفَّانِ, two sandals.
  • Learn the real story behind three proverbs still used in Arabic today, and the exact moral each one carries.
  • Connect a broken promise to the authentic hadith on the signs of a hypocrite.

The Arabs have countless أَمْثَال — proverbs, each one carrying a whole story folded into a single sentence. Some proverbs come from before Islam, some from the early Islamic centuries. This lesson tells you three of the most famous, with the full قِصَّة (story) behind each one — so that next time you hear the proverb alone, you will know exactly what it is quietly saying.

خُفَّانِ is your first real look at المُثَنَّى, the dual — Arabic's third number, sitting between singular and plural for exactly two of something. One sandal is خُفّ; the pair Ḥunayn made are خُفَّانِ in rafʿ, خُفَّيْنِ in naṣb and jarr — the same اَنِ / يْنِ ending you will meet again and again once you start counting things in twos.

١ — جَزَاءُ سِنِمَّار Proverb 1 — "The Reward of Sinimmār"

Al-Nuʿmān, king of al-Ḥīrah, wanted to build himself a magnificent palace, so he chose a master builder named Sinimmār. Sinimmār built him a palace of astonishing beauty, and the king was overjoyed with it. One day al-Nuʿmān asked Sinimmār to walk with him around the palace, all the way up to its roof. There he asked him: "Is there a palace like this anywhere?" Sinimmār said: "No." "Could another builder make one like it?" "No," said Sinimmār. Hearing this, al-Nuʿmān thought to himself: if this man lives, he will build someone else a palace even finer than mine. So he ordered his soldiers to throw Sinimmār from the roof, and he died. Since then, «جَزَاءُ سِنِمَّار» — "the reward of Sinimmār" — is said of anyone who repays a good deed with evil.

٢ — رَجَعَ بِخُفَّيْ حُنَيْن Proverb 2 — "He Returned with Ḥunayn's Two Sandals"

Ḥunayn was a cobbler in al-Ḥīrah. One day a bedouin came to buy a pair of sandals from him and haggled over the price until he angered him. Ḥunayn decided to teach him a lesson: as the bedouin was riding home, Ḥunayn threw one sandal in his path, and a little further on, threw the second. The bedouin found the first sandal and said to himself: "This looks just like Ḥunayn's sandal! If only I had the other one." He rode on — and found the second. Regretting that he had left the first behind, he got down from his camel, left it there, and walked back for the first sandal. While he was gone, Ḥunayn — who had been watching from hiding the whole time — took the camel and left. The bedouin returned home with nothing but two sandals and no camel. Since then, «رَجَعَ بِخُفَّيْ حُنَيْن» is said of anyone who comes back empty-handed, having lost everything through his own greed.

٣ — مَوَاعِيدُ عُرْقُوب Proverb 3 — "The Promises of ʿUrqūb"

ʿUrqūb was a man known for breaking every promise he made. His brother once came asking him for help, so ʿUrqūb said: "When this palm tree flowers, its fruit is yours." When it flowered, his brother came back — so ʿUrqūb said: "Leave it until it ripens to red." When it ripened, his brother came again — "Leave it until it becomes soft dates." When it did — "Leave it until it becomes dried dates." And when the dates were finally ready, ʿUrqūb went out at night, picked the whole crop for himself, and gave his brother nothing. Since then, «مَوَاعِيدُ عُرْقُوب» is said of anyone who keeps breaking his word.

حديث — a proverb's real weight

«آيَةُ الْمُنَافِقِ ثَلَاثٌ: إِذَا حَدَّثَ كَذَبَ، وَإِذَا وَعَدَ أَخْلَفَ، وَإِذَا اؤْتُمِنَ خَانَ»

"The sign of the hypocrite is three: when he speaks he lies, when he promises he breaks it (أَخْلَفَ), and when he is trusted he betrays" (al-Bukhārī & Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah). ʿUrqūb's proverb is not just an old story — Islam names breaking a promise a sign of nifāq (hypocrisy). The word أَخْلَفَ in the hadith is the exact verb your table just taught you.

تمرين أسري — Family Practice

Retell all three proverb-stories around the table without looking at the page, then ask each other: has anyone ever kept a promise to you the way ʿUrqūb didn't — or have you ever been tempted to "leave it until next time" the way he did? Close with the hadith of the three signs, and let each person name which of the three (lying, breaking promises, betraying trust) is hardest for them personally to guard.
