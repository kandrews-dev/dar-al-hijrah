المُسَاوَاةُ الحَقَّة

True Equality

Objectives — أهداف الدرس

  • Know the vocabulary of justice and equality — from مُسَاوَاةٌ to قِصَاصٌ.
  • Meet two more broken plurals — حَدّ ← حُدُود and حُرّ ← أَحْرَار — on two more unrelated patterns.
  • Know the authentic hadith of the Makhzūmiyyah woman: equal ḥudūd for everyone, without exception.
  • Meet ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb's famous stand for a wronged commoner against a governor's son.

Islam declared مُسَاوَاة — equality — as a founding principle, centuries before the modern world's declarations of it, and unlike those declarations, this one was not left as theory. The Prophet ﷺ and his Companions lived it, in the row of ṣalāh, in the enforcement of the law, and in how a ruler answered for his own mistakes. This lesson reads real, verified accounts of what true equality looked like in practice.

Two broken plurals sit side by side in this table, and they prove the same point twice: حَدّ becomes حُدُود on the فُعُول pattern, exactly like عَقْل ← عُقُول from Vocab 30. But حُرّ becomes أَحْرَار on a completely different pattern, أَفْعَال. There is no single broken-plural rule to memorise once and apply everywhere — each noun is learned on its own, the way you are learning both of these right now.

المساواة في الصلاة Equality in Prayer

The source text points first to something every Muslim has stood inside: the row of ṣalāh. The small and the great stand shoulder to shoulder, the rich and the poor, every colour of mankind, all facing one qiblah and praying to one God — no reserved front row for rank or wealth. The Prophet ﷺ commanded the rows themselves be kept straight and unbroken:

«سَوُّوا صُفُوفَكُمْ فَإِنَّ تَسْوِيَةَ الصَّفِّ مِنْ تَمَامِ الصَّلَاةِ»

"Straighten your rows, for straightening the row is part of the completeness of the prayer" (al-Bukhārī & Muslim, from Anas ibn Mālik). One straight line, no one ahead by rank — the same مُسَاوَاة the source text points to appears in the very shape of the row you stand in five times a day.

تنفيذ الحدود على الجميع Ḥudūd Enforced on Everyone

A woman from Banū Makhzūm — a noble tribe — was found to have stolen. Her family, fearing the ḥadd punishment, sought out Usāmah ibn Zayd, whom the Prophet ﷺ loved dearly, to intercede for her. When Usāmah spoke to the Prophet ﷺ about it, his face changed in anger.

«أَتَشْفَعُ فِي حَدٍّ مِنْ حُدُودِ اللَّهِ؟» ثُمَّ قَامَ فَخَطَبَ فَقَالَ: «إِنَّمَا أَهْلَكَ الَّذِينَ قَبْلَكُمْ أَنَّهُمْ كَانُوا إِذَا سَرَقَ فِيهِمُ الشَّرِيفُ تَرَكُوهُ، وَإِذَا سَرَقَ فِيهِمُ الضَّعِيفُ أَقَامُوا عَلَيْهِ الْحَدَّ، وَايْمُ اللَّهِ لَوْ أَنَّ فَاطِمَةَ بِنْتَ مُحَمَّدٍ سَرَقَتْ لَقَطَعْتُ يَدَهَا»

"Are you interceding regarding one of the ḥudūd (legal limits) of Allah?" Then he stood and delivered a sermon, saying: "Those before you were destroyed only because when a noble person among them stole, they would let him go, but when a weak person among them stole, they would carry out the ḥadd on him. By Allah, if Fāṭimah, the daughter of Muḥammad, were to steal, I would cut off her hand" (al-Bukhārī & Muslim, from ʿĀʼishah).

Notice حُدُودِ اللَّهِ — the broken plural from your table, now doing real legal work: not one law for the noble and another for the weak, but one حَدّ for every hand that steals, even — the Prophet ﷺ said — his own daughter's.

مَتَى اسْتَعْبَدْتُمُ النَّاسَ "Since When Have You Enslaved People?"

A well-known account from the khilāfah of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb: the son of ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, governor of Egypt, struck an Egyptian man, relying on his father's authority. The man travelled to Madīnah and complained to ʿUmar, who summoned ʿAmr and his son and had the man strike the son back in equal measure. Then ʿUmar turned to ʿAmr and said his famous line:

«يَا عَمْرُو، مَتَى اسْتَعْبَدْتُمُ النَّاسَ وَقَدْ وَلَدَتْهُمْ أُمَّهَاتُهُمْ أَحْرَارًا؟»

"O ʿAmr, since when have you enslaved people, when their mothers bore them free (أَحْرَارًا)?" This is a famous historical report (athar), recorded across classical histories such as Ibn ʿAbd al-Ḥakam's Futūḥ Miṣr — not a hadith of the Prophet ﷺ, but the enduring standard it set: a governor's own son answers to the same law as anyone else, and rank buys no one out of قِصَاص.

مساءلة الخليفة نفسه Even the Khalīfah Answers

The source text also records ʿUmar wronging a man himself, striking him without full right — then, once he realised, calling the man back, handing him the whip, and telling him: "Strike me as I struck you." The man refused and forgave him for Allah's sake. ʿUmar went home, prayed two rakʿahs, then sat alone rebuking himself: "O Ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, you were lowly and Allah raised you, misguided and Allah guided you, weak and Allah made you strong and made you khalīfah — and a man came seeking your help against injustice, and you wronged him! What will you say to your Lord when you meet Him tomorrow?" — until, the account says, the people around him felt sorrow for how hard he was on himself. This is the same مُسَاوَاة turned inward: a khalīfah who does not exempt his own hand from the standard he enforces on everyone else's.

تمرين أسري — Family Practice

As a family, retell ʿUmar's two stories in your own words — the man he wronged, and the son of ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ — then ask: what does it cost a leader to hold himself to the same standard as everyone else? Close by standing shoulder to shoulder for a moment, the way a ṣaff of ṣalāh stands, and recall «سَوُّوا صُفُوفَكُمْ».

Daily Commitment — التزام يومي

لَوْ أَنَّ فَاطِمَةَ بِنْتَ مُحَمَّدٍ سَرَقَتْ لَقَطَعْتُ يَدَهَا

Memorize this line until it is part of you — "If Fāṭimah, the daughter of Muḥammad, were to steal, I would cut off her hand" (al-Bukhārī & Muslim, from ʿĀʼishah) — the standard of equal justice, spoken by the Messenger ﷺ himself.
