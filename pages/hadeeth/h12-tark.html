Ḥadīth 12 of 42 · al-Tirmidhī · ḥasan

تَرْكُهُ مَا لَا يَعْنِيهِ

Leaving What Does Not Concern You

Objectives — أهداف الدرس

  • Memorize the ḥadīth of أبو هريرة: from the excellence of a person's Islam is his leaving what does not concern him.
  • Understand حُسْن الإسلام (excellence of Islam) — that Islam has levels, and this ḥadīth is a door to الإحسان from Ḥadīth 2.
  • Learn the measure of مَا لَا يَعْنِيهِ: the شريعة, not personal whim — and why this ḥadīth is never an excuse to abandon commanding good.
  • Identify the two biggest leaks — the tongue and the eyes — and Ibn Rajab's warning about the tongue's harvest.
  • Reclaim the time payoff: turn hours lost to what doesn't concern you into memorisation and revision as a طالب علم (student of knowledge).

عَنْ أَبِي هُرَيْرَةَ رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ قَالَ: قَالَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ ﷺ: «مِنْ حُسْنِ إِسْلَامِ الْمَرْءِ تَرْكُهُ مَا لَا يَعْنِيهِ» حَدِيثٌ حَسَنٌ، رَوَاهُ التِّرْمِذِيُّ وَغَيْرُهُ هَكَذَا.

On the authority of Abū Hurayrah (may Allah be pleased with him) who said: The Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: "From the excellence of a person's Islam is his leaving what does not concern him." A ḥasan ḥadīth, narrated by al-Tirmidhī and others in this wording.

Narrated by Abū Hurayrah · al-Tirmidhī · ḥasan

الراوي أبو هريرة The Narrator — Abū Hurayrah

أبو هريرة (Abū Hurayrah) — عبد الرحمن بن صخر الدوسي — is the most prolific narrator among all the Companions: over five thousand ḥadīths carry his name. Yet he only accompanied the Prophet ﷺ for about four years, coming to Madinah in the year of خيبر. How did four years produce such an ocean? Because he lived this very ḥadīth. While others were busy in the markets and the fields, he chose the صُفَّة — the shaded platform of the mosque — hungry, patient, and glued to the Prophet ﷺ, leaving everything that did not concern his purpose. He himself said that his brothers among the المهاجرون were occupied with trade and the الأنصار with their farms, while he stayed present for what they missed. The Prophet ﷺ made duʿāʾ for his memory, and he never forgot what he heard after it. His famous kunyah — "father of the little cat" — came from a kitten he used to carry. When the man who narrated the most tells you the excellence of Islam lies in leaving something, listen carefully: he built his mountain of knowledge on exactly that leaving.

حسن الإسلام درجات — باب إلى الإحسان Excellence in Islam Has Levels — a Door to Iḥsān

The ḥadīth does not say "from Islam" but مِنْ حُسْنِ إِسْلَامِ الْمَرْءِ — from the excellence of a person's Islam. Ibn Rajab explains in جامع العلوم والحكم that people's Islam is not one flat grade: there is the bare minimum that keeps a person inside the religion, and above it rising degrees of beauty and completeness. Two people pray the same prayer and fast the same month, yet one's Islam is أحسن (more excellent) than the other's — because of what fills, and what is kept out of, the rest of his day.

Where does the ladder lead? To الإحسان — the summit you met in Ḥadīth 2, when جبريل asked and the Prophet ﷺ answered: to worship Allah as though you see Him. Shaykh al-ʿUthaymīn points out the connection: the one who knows Allah is watching does not spend his hours poking through matters that are none of his business — his sense of being seen trims his talk, his glances, and his curiosity. So this short ḥadīth is a practical door into الإحسان: you cannot climb toward "worship as though you see Him" while your tongue and eyes wander wherever they please. Leaving مَا لَا يَعْنِيهِ is how a person's Islam starts to become beautiful.

ما لا يعنيه — بميزان الشريعة لا الهوى "What Does Not Concern Him" — Measured by the Sharīʿah, Not by Whim

Here is where many people misread the ḥadīth. Who decides what concerns you? Not your mood, not your curiosity, not "I just find it interesting" — the شريعة decides. Ibn Rajab and Shaykh al-ʿUthaymīn both stress this: مَا يَعْنِيكَ (what concerns you) is whatever benefits your دين (religion) and your دنيا (worldly life) in a way the شريعة approves — your worship, your knowledge, your family, your honest earnings, your health. And مَا لَا يَعْنِيكَ is what brings you no such benefit: other people's private affairs, prying questions, arguments that produce nothing, news you can neither act on nor learn from. If the measure were personal whim, every busybody would declare that gossip "concerns" him — for the نفس finds it fascinating! The شريعة, not the appetite, draws the line.

And the scholars close a second door of misuse: this ḥadīth is never an excuse to abandon الأمر بالمعروف والنهي عن المنكر (commanding good and forbidding evil). "It's not my business what he does" is not الورع — it is neglect dressed up as piety. You already learned in Ḥadīth 7 that الدين النصيحة — the religion itself is sincere counsel, including النصيحة to the common Muslims. So your brother's guidance is your concern; the شريعة made it so. What does not concern you is his private matters that carry no ruling and no rescue — spying, speculation, and chatter about him. Feel the difference: النصيحة walks toward your brother to benefit him; الفضول (nosiness) circles him to feed on him.

اللسان والعين — أكبر منفذ The Tongue and the Eyes — the Biggest Leak

If you audited a week of your life for مَا لَا يَعْنِيكَ, where would most of it be found? Ibn Rajab answers: on the tongue. Most of what does not concern a person is his tongue's harvest — the حَصَائِد (harvests) his speech sows and his record reaps. Count the forms: the prying question ("how much did you pay? why did they divorce?"), the idle comparison, the relayed rumour, the opinion nobody asked for about a matter you cannot change. None of it is necessarily حرام on its face — that is precisely the trap. It leaks reward drop by drop and fills the record page by page, while the speaker thinks he has done nothing at all.

The eyes are the tongue's partner in the leak — and never more than today. The scrolling feed is فضول of the eyes: hours of watching lives that are none of your business, quarrels you are not part of, and news that neither teaches you your religion nor earns you your bread. Shaykh al-ʿUthaymīn notes that a person is only asked about what he was given charge of — so why volunteer to carry the affairs of a thousand strangers? The remedy the ḥadīth offers is not a harsh vow of silence; it is a filter. Before you speak, type, or click: does this benefit my دين or my دنيا by the measure of the شريعة? If yes, proceed with excellence. If no — تَرْكُهُ: leave it, and know that the leaving itself is worship, counted from the حُسْن of your Islam.

مكسب الوقت لطالب العلم The Time Payoff for the Student of Knowledge

Now turn the ḥadīth from a shield into a tool. Every hour reclaimed from مَا لَا يَعْنِيكَ does not disappear — it becomes vacant, and the طالب علم (student of knowledge) knows exactly how to fill a vacant hour. The scholars of the salaf were miserly with time the way others are miserly with gold: they understood that the memoriser's real enemy is not the difficulty of the text but the leak in the day. Twenty minutes not spent on a stranger's quarrel is twenty minutes of القرآن review; the half-hour not scrolled away after فجر is a half-hour in which vocabulary sticks best. You have already begun building this habit in the Arabic track's lesson on طلب العلم — the vocabulary of seeking knowledge. This ḥadīth is its guardian: طلب العلم fills the hours, and تَرْكُ مَا لَا يَعْنِي protects them. Try it this week: each time you catch yourself reaching for what does not concern you, trade the moment for one flashcard, one āyah, one line of revision — and watch what a month of traded moments builds.

A gem from Ibn Rajab — فائدة

Ibn Rajab relates in جامع العلوم والحكم that the imams counted this ḥadīth among the small handful of comprehensive sayings upon which the whole religion turns — a quarter of Islam, some said, alongside the ḥadīth of النية. Why so much weight in nine words? Because the religion has two halves: doing what benefits, and leaving what harms or distracts — and this ḥadīth is the entire second half in a single sentence. Notice also its gentleness: it does not thunder "forbidden!" at every idle word; it simply tells you what the beautiful Islam looks like, and lets your own longing for حُسْن pull you upward. The ḥadīths of الورع you met before — Ḥadīth 6 on the doubtful and Ḥadīth 11 on leaving what makes you doubt — guard the boundary of the حرام; this one goes further and beautifies even the حلال hours. That is the ladder: sound, then safe, then beautiful.
