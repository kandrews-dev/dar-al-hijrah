Ḥadīth 6 of 42 · Agreed upon

الحَلَالُ بَيِّنٌ وَالحَرَامُ بَيِّنٌ

The Lawful, the Prohibited & the Doubtful Matters

Objectives — أهداف الدرس

  • Memorize the ḥadīth of النعمان بن بشير: the halal is clear, the haram is clear, and between them are doubtful matters.
  • Understand the three categories of matters and why الشبهات (doubtful matters) are hidden from many people but not from the scholars.
  • Learn الورع (scrupulousness) — how leaving the doubtful protects your religion and your honour.
  • Unpack the parable of the shepherd and الحمى (the protected sanctuary), and what حمى الله is.
  • See the heart as the king of the limbs — and how this ḥadīth completes the pairing of Ḥadīth 1 (the inward) and Ḥadīth 5 (the outward).

عَنْ أَبِي عَبْدِ اللَّهِ النُّعْمَانِ بْنِ بَشِيرٍ رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُمَا قَالَ: سَمِعْتُ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ ﷺ يَقُولُ: «إِنَّ الْحَلَالَ بَيِّنٌ وَإِنَّ الْحَرَامَ بَيِّنٌ، وَبَيْنَهُمَا أُمُورٌ مُشْتَبِهَاتٌ لَا يَعْلَمُهُنَّ كَثِيرٌ مِنَ النَّاسِ، فَمَنِ اتَّقَى الشُّبُهَاتِ فَقَدِ اسْتَبْرَأَ لِدِينِهِ وَعِرْضِهِ، وَمَنْ وَقَعَ فِي الشُّبُهَاتِ وَقَعَ فِي الْحَرَامِ، كَالرَّاعِي يَرْعَى حَوْلَ الْحِمَى يُوشِكُ أَنْ يَرْتَعَ فِيهِ، أَلَا وَإِنَّ لِكُلِّ مَلِكٍ حِمًى، أَلَا وَإِنَّ حِمَى اللَّهِ مَحَارِمُهُ، أَلَا وَإِنَّ فِي الْجَسَدِ مُضْغَةً إِذَا صَلَحَتْ صَلَحَ الْجَسَدُ كُلُّهُ، وَإِذَا فَسَدَتْ فَسَدَ الْجَسَدُ كُلُّهُ، أَلَا وَهِيَ الْقَلْبُ» رَوَاهُ الْبُخَارِيُّ وَمُسْلِمٌ.

Abū ʿAbdillāh al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr (may Allah be pleased with them both) said: I heard the Messenger of Allah ﷺ say: "Truly the halal is clear and truly the haram is clear, and between them are doubtful matters not known to many people. So whoever guards against the doubtful has protected his religion and his honour, and whoever falls into the doubtful falls into the haram — like the shepherd grazing around a protected sanctuary, about to graze within it. Truly every king has a sanctuary, and truly Allah's sanctuary is His prohibitions. And truly in the body is a morsel of flesh: when it is sound the whole body is sound, and when it is corrupt the whole body is corrupt — indeed it is the heart." Narrated by al-Bukhārī and Muslim.

Narrated by al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr · al-Bukhārī & Muslim

الراوي النعمان بن بشير The Narrator — al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr

النعمان بن بشير (al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr) was among the first children born to the الأنصار (the Helpers of Madinah) after the hijrah. Notice the wording رضي الله عنهما — "may Allah be pleased with them both" — because his father بشير بن سعد was also a Companion, so father and son share the duʿāʾ. Though young in the Prophet's ﷺ lifetime, he heard from him directly: in Muslim's narration of this very ḥadīth he points with two fingers to his ears as he says "I heard the Messenger of Allah ﷺ." He later became a governor and a preacher who would deliver these words from the minbar, and he is also the narrator of the famous ḥadīth commanding fathers to treat their children with equal fairness. Through him Allah preserved for us one of the greatest foundations of the religion: a single ḥadīth that maps out the halal, the haram, the doubtful — and the heart that steers a person through all three.

ثلث الإسلام — أقسام الأمور ثلاثة A Third of Islam — the Three Categories of Matters

Ibn Rajab relates in جامع العلوم والحكم that Imam أحمد said Islam revolves around three ḥadīths: the ḥadīth of النية (Ḥadīth 1), the ḥadīth of رد البدعة (Ḥadīth 5), and this ḥadīth — so the scholars called it a third of Islam. Why so mighty? Because it sorts everything a person could ever do or take into three categories. First, the clear halal: bread, fruit, honest trade, truthful speech — بَيِّنٌ (clear), needing no scholar to uncover it. Second, the clear haram: الربا (usury), الخمر (intoxicants), lying, backbiting — equally plain. Third, between the two lie أُمُورٌ مُشْتَبِهَاتٌ (doubtful matters): matters whose evidences resemble one another or whose ruling is hidden, so that the halal and the haram in them are not obvious.

Listen closely to the wording: لَا يَعْلَمُهُنَّ كَثِيرٌ مِنَ النَّاسِ — "not known to many people." It does not say "not known to anyone." Shaykh al-ʿUthaymīn draws out the implication: the doubtful matters are known to the firmly-grounded scholars, who trace them back to the Book and the Sunnah until the ruling becomes clear. So a شبهة (doubt) is not a permanent fog — it is fog for you, until you ask the people of knowledge. The ḥadīth therefore teaches two duties at once: caution for the layman, and asking for the seeker.

الورع — استبرأ لدينه وعرضه Scrupulousness — Protecting Your Religion and Your Honour

فَمَنِ اتَّقَى الشُّبُهَاتِ — "whoever guards against the doubtful" — فَقَدِ اسْتَبْرَأَ لِدِينِهِ وَعِرْضِهِ: he has sought clearance for two precious things at once. For his دِين (religion), before Allah — he has kept his worship, his earnings, and his food free of what may secretly be haram. And for his عِرْض (honour), before people — he has given no one cause to suspect him, for the one seen wading in murky matters invites tongues to wag even if he is innocent. This deliberate stepping back from the grey zone is what the scholars call الوَرَع (scrupulousness): leaving what may harm you for what certainly cannot.

And the other side: وَمَنْ وَقَعَ فِي الشُّبُهَاتِ وَقَعَ فِي الْحَرَامِ — "whoever falls into the doubtful falls into the haram." Ibn Rajab explains this in two ways, and both are true. Either the doubtful thing was in reality haram, so the one who took it fell into haram without knowing; or the habit of grazing on doubtful things slowly deadens the heart's caution, so that the step from grey into black becomes small and easy. Either way, the man who treats the boundary casually will cross it — which is exactly the picture the Prophet ﷺ paints next.

مثل الراعي والحمى The Parable of the Shepherd and the Sanctuary

The kings of Arabia would set aside rich pasture as a حِمًى — a protected sanctuary fenced off by decree, where no outsider's flock could graze, on pain of punishment. Now picture the shepherd who leads his sheep right up to its edge and grazes them around the boundary. Hungry animals do not read boundary lines: يُوشِكُ أَنْ يَرْتَعَ فِيهِ — "he is about to graze within it." He may not intend to trespass; he has simply put himself where trespass is one stray step away. The Prophet ﷺ then turns the key: أَلَا وَإِنَّ حِمَى اللَّهِ مَحَارِمُهُ — every king has a sanctuary, and Allah's sanctuary is His prohibitions.

The doubtful matters are the grass at the edge of the fence. The one who circles them — doubtful income, doubtful gatherings, doubtful glances, "it's not technically haram" — is the shepherd at the boundary, and his nafs is the hungry flock. Shaykh al-ʿUthaymīn notes that this is why the Sharīʿah does not only forbid the haram but blocks the roads leading to it: the wise believer does not ask "how close can I get?" but "how clear can I stay?" Distance from the fence is not lost profit — it is the very protection the ḥadīth promised: لدينه وعرضه, for his religion and his honour.

القلب ملك الجوارح The Heart — King of the Limbs

The ḥadīth closes with its deepest line: in the body is a مُضْغَة (a morsel of flesh — literally, as much as one chews) — when it is sound the whole body is sound, and when it is corrupt the whole body is corrupt — أَلَا وَهِيَ الْقَلْبُ: indeed it is the heart. You met this very word مضغة in Ḥadīth 4, naming the tiny lump of flesh we each once were in the womb; and the Arabic track's body-parts lesson crowns القلب as the greatest limb with this same closing clause. Why does the whole ḥadīth about halal and haram end at the heart? Because the limbs are soldiers and the heart is their king: the eye does not stray, the hand does not take, and the foot does not walk to the doubtful except by the heart's command. A person only dares graze at the edge of the fence when the fence has already fallen inside him.

This is also where the ḥadīth joins what you learned in Islam 101 about الإحسان — worshipping Allah as though you see Him. الإحسان lives nowhere but the heart; and a heart that watches Allah does not need a scholar standing over every doubtful matter, because its own الورع pulls it back from the fence. So the reform of the heart — filling it with الإخلاص (sincerity), knowledge, and fear of Allah — is not one duty among many. It is the duty on which every other duty depends.

A gem from Ibn Rajab — فائدة

Ibn Rajab draws the three great ḥadīths together into one frame. Ḥadīth 1 — إنما الأعمال بالنيات — is the inward scale: no deed counts unless it is for Allah. Ḥadīth 5 — من أحدث في أمرنا — is the outward scale: no deed counts unless it follows the Messenger ﷺ. And this ḥadīth names the engine of both: the القلب. Sincerity is an act of the heart; the resolve to follow the Sunnah and to stop at the boundaries is an act of the heart. When the مضغة is sound, the inward scale and the outward scale both come out true — and when it is corrupt, no amount of outward polish can save the deed. So the first workshop of the student of knowledge is his own chest.

Memorize & Renew — احفظ وجدّد

أَلَا وَإِنَّ فِي الْجَسَدِ مُضْغَةً إِذَا صَلَحَتْ صَلَحَ الْجَسَدُ كُلُّهُ، وَإِذَا فَسَدَتْ فَسَدَ الْجَسَدُ كُلُّهُ، أَلَا وَهِيَ الْقَلْبُ

"And truly in the body is a morsel of flesh: when it is sound the whole body is sound, and when it is corrupt the whole body is corrupt — indeed it is the heart." Memorize it word-perfect, then work the whole ḥadīth into your memory clause by clause. And each time you meet a grey area, remember the shepherd at the fence — and ask your heart which way it is leaning.
