Ḥadīth 13 of 42 · Agreed upon

لَا يُؤْمِنُ أَحَدُكُمْ حَتَّى يُحِبَّ لِأَخِيهِ مَا يُحِبُّ لِنَفْسِهِ

Loving for Your Brother What You Love for Yourself

Objectives — أهداف الدرس

  • Memorize the ḥadīth of أنس بن مالك: none of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself.
  • Meet the narrator — the Prophet's ﷺ خادم (servant) for ten years, to whom he never once said "uff."
  • Learn the scholars' rule for لَا يُؤْمِنُ: it negates the completeness of الإيمان (faith), not its existence — and how this guards against two extremes.
  • Understand سلامة الصدر (soundness of heart) as the root of this love, and its enemy الحسد (envy).
  • Test yourself against three practical measures: duʿāʾ for your brother, his success in study, and honesty in trade.

عَنْ أَبِي حَمْزَةَ أَنَسِ بْنِ مَالِكٍ رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ، خَادِمِ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ ﷺ، عَنِ النَّبِيِّ ﷺ قَالَ: «لَا يُؤْمِنُ أَحَدُكُمْ حَتَّى يُحِبَّ لِأَخِيهِ مَا يُحِبُّ لِنَفْسِهِ» رَوَاهُ الْبُخَارِيُّ وَمُسْلِمٌ.

Abū Ḥamzah Anas ibn Mālik (may Allah be pleased with him), the servant of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said: "None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself." Narrated by al-Bukhārī and Muslim.

Narrated by Anas ibn Mālik · al-Bukhārī & Muslim · Agreed upon

الراوي أنس بن مالك The Narrator — Anas ibn Mālik

When the Prophet ﷺ arrived in Madinah, a mother of the الأنصار (the Helpers) brought him a gift unlike any other: her own son. "O Messenger of Allah, this is أنس (Anas) — let him serve you." He was about ten years old, and for the next ten years he was the Prophet's ﷺ خادم (servant) — in his house, on his journeys, at his side. Listen to how Anas himself summed up those years: "I served him ten years, and he never once said to me uff — never said about a thing I did, 'why did you do it?', nor about a thing I left, 'why did you not do it?'" Who could speak about love between believers with more right than the boy who lived inside the Prophet's ﷺ own gentleness for a decade? The Prophet ﷺ made duʿāʾ for him — abundant wealth, children, and a long life — and Allah answered all of it: Anas lived past ninety, one of the very last Companions to die, and became one of the most prolific narrators of the Sunnah.

لا يؤمن — نفي الكمال لا نفي الأصل "None of You Believes" — Negating Perfection, Not Existence

The ḥadīth opens with a thunderclap: لَا يُؤْمِنُ أَحَدُكُمْ — "none of you believes…" Does this mean that a Muslim who falls short in loving for his brother has left Islam altogether? No — and here the scholars give you a rule you will use again and again. Ibn Rajab and Shaykh al-ʿUthaymīn both explain: what is negated here is the كمال (completeness, perfection) of الإيمان, not its أصل (root, existence). The one who does not love for his brother what he loves for himself is a believer of deficient faith — his إيمان is real but wounded, like a tree that lives yet bears thin fruit.

This rule only makes sense because of something you learned in the Aqeedah track: الإيمان increases with obedience and decreases with sin. If faith were a single on/off switch, "none of you believes until…" could only mean expulsion from Islam. Because faith is a rising and falling reality, the ḥadīth can say: your faith is less until this love fills it. And notice how the rule guards against both extremes: against the extremism of those who throw Muslims out of Islam for their sins and shortcomings, and against the laxity of those who say "faith is just in the heart, deeds don't touch it — so relax." The straight path of أهل السنة runs between the two: the shortcoming is serious enough to wound your faith, yet not so total as to erase it.

ميزان الأخوة — سلامة الصدر The Brotherhood Standard — Soundness of Heart

What exactly must you love for your brother? Everything good you love for yourself — the good of the دين (religion) first: that he prays with khushūʿ as you wish to, memorizes as you wish to, is guided, forgiven, and admitted to Paradise as you wish to be. And the good of the دنيا (worldly life) too: health, provision, a righteous family, ease in his affairs. The ḥadīth hands you a scale you carry everywhere — before you speak of your brother, deal with him, or compete with him, weigh it: would I love this for myself?

Ibn Rajab in جامع العلوم والحكم takes you to the root of the matter: this love cannot live in a chest occupied by الحسد (envy) — the wish that your brother's blessing would leave him. The two are opposites: the envier grieves when his brother is given good, while the possessor of this ḥadīth rejoices as if the gift were his own. So the real workshop of this ḥadīth is سلامة الصدر (soundness of heart) — a chest swept clean of غِلّ (rancour) and حسد, which the Salaf counted among the lightest of deeds on the limbs and the heaviest on the scale. You met الحسد and its opposite among the heart-words of the feelings vocabulary lesson in the Arabic track — now you see the ḥadīth those words were preparing you for. Whoever finds envy stirring should fight it: make duʿāʾ for the one he envies, speak well of him, and give to him — until the heart follows the limbs into soundness.

اختبارات عملية Practical Tests — Does Your Heart Pass?

A ḥadīth like this is easy to nod at and hard to live. So test yourself with three measures drawn from daily life. First — the duʿāʾ test. When did you last ask Allah, in your brother's absence, for the very things you ask for yourself? The Prophet ﷺ taught that the duʿāʾ of a Muslim for his brother بظهر الغيب (in his absence) is answered, and the angel says: "and for you the same." The one who loves for his brother what he loves for himself does this naturally — his duʿāʾ list is longer than his own needs.

Second — the study-circle test. In the مجلس العلم (gathering of knowledge) — like the circle you read about in the Arabic reading lesson on the majlis — your classmate memorizes faster than you. Does your chest tighten, or do you rejoice and want his ḥifẓ to grow as you want your own? Wanting your brother's success in memorization and understanding, and helping him toward it, is this ḥadīth walking on two feet in the madrasah.

Third — the marketplace test. You are selling something with a hidden defect. Would you want the seller to disclose it to you? Then disclose it. The honest merchant of the wealth-and-trade vocabulary lesson is simply this ḥadīth applied to المال (wealth): whatever disclosure, fair price, and easy terms you would love for yourself, love them — and give them — to your brother. A trader who passes this test has made his shop a place of عبادة (worship).

A gem from Ibn Rajab — فائدة

Set this ḥadīth beside Ḥadīth 7 — الدين النصيحة — and you will find two faces of one coin. النصيحة (sincere goodwill) to the common Muslims is the outward face: guiding them to good, teaching them, defending them in their absence. This ḥadīth is the inward face: the love in the chest from which all that naṣīḥah flows. A man cannot sincerely advise people whose good he does not love, and he cannot truly love their good without it spilling into advice, help, and duʿāʾ. So Ḥadīth 13 plants the tree and Ḥadīth 7 picks its fruit — and both grow only in the soil of سلامة الصدر.

Memorize & Renew — احفظ وجدّد

لَا يُؤْمِنُ أَحَدُكُمْ حَتَّى يُحِبَّ لِأَخِيهِ مَا يُحِبُّ لِنَفْسِهِ

"None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself." One short line — memorize it word-perfect today. Then renew it daily: each time good comes to a brother of yours, catch your heart in the act and make it say الحمد لله as though the good were your own — and add him to your duʿāʾ in his absence.
