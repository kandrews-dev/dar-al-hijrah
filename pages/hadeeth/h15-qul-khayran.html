Ḥadīth 15 of 42 · Agreed upon

فَلْيَقُلْ خَيْرًا أَوْ لِيَصْمُتْ

Speak Good or Stay Silent — the Neighbour & the Guest

Objectives — أهداف الدرس

  • Memorize the ḥadīth of أبو هريرة: speak good or remain silent, honour the neighbour, honour the guest.
  • Understand why every command is prefaced with مَنْ كَانَ يُؤْمِنُ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ — īmān as the engine of conduct.
  • Learn the tongue rule: weigh your words before they leave — if good, say them; if not, silence itself is worship.
  • Know the rights of الجار (the neighbour) in Islam, and the narration of Jibrīl's repeated advice about him.
  • Understand إكرام الضيف (honouring the guest) and connect it to the Arabic track's hospitality lesson.

عَنْ أَبِي هُرَيْرَةَ رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ أَنَّ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ ﷺ قَالَ: «مَنْ كَانَ يُؤْمِنُ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ فَلْيَقُلْ خَيْرًا أَوْ لِيَصْمُتْ، وَمَنْ كَانَ يُؤْمِنُ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ فَلْيُكْرِمْ جَارَهُ، وَمَنْ كَانَ يُؤْمِنُ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ فَلْيُكْرِمْ ضَيْفَهُ» رَوَاهُ الْبُخَارِيُّ وَمُسْلِمٌ.

Abū Hurayrah (may Allah be pleased with him) narrated that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: "Whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day, let him speak good or remain silent; and whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day, let him honour his neighbour; and whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day, let him honour his guest." Narrated by al-Bukhārī and Muslim.

Narrated by Abū Hurayrah · al-Bukhārī & Muslim · Agreed upon

الراوي أبو هريرة The Narrator — Abū Hurayrah

أبو هريرة (Abū Hurayrah) — عبد الرحمن بن صخر الدوسي — is the most prolific narrator among all the Companions, preserving for the ummah over five thousand ḥadīths. He came to the Prophet ﷺ from the tribe of دوس in the year of Khaybar and then never left his side: while others were occupied with trade and farmland, he chose the الصُّفَّة (the shaded platform of the mosque where the poorest Companions lived) so that he could attend every sitting and miss nothing. He once complained of a weak memory, so the Prophet ﷺ told him to spread out his cloak, made a gesture into it, and told him to gather it to his chest — and he said: "I never forgot anything after that." His famous kunyah — "father of the little kitten" — came from a cat he used to carry and care for; even his nickname tells you something of the gentleness this ḥadīth commands. It is fitting that the Companion who guarded the Prophet's ﷺ words most carefully is the one who transmits the ḥadīth about guarding your own.

من كان يؤمن بالله واليوم الآخر — الإيمان محرّك العمل The Refrain — Īmān as the Engine of Conduct

Before each of the three commands, the Prophet ﷺ repeats one refrain: مَنْ كَانَ يُؤْمِنُ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ — "whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day." He did not simply say "speak good" and "honour your neighbour"; he hung every command on الإيمان (faith) itself. Shaykh al-ʿUthaymīn explains the meaning: whoever truly believes in Allah — knowing He hears every word — and in الْيَوْم الْآخِر (the Last Day), knowing every word and deed will be laid out before him then, let that belief show in how he speaks and how he treats the people nearest him. The conditional form is a spur, not a threat of kufr: it means these manners are the fruit of faith, so a believer who neglects them has left his īmān incomplete in that measure.

Notice which two articles of faith are chosen. بِاللَّهِ — because He is watching you now, as you open your mouth or your door. وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ — because you will be asked then, when the record is spread open. You studied that Day in the Aqeedah track's lesson on اليوم الآخر; this ḥadīth is where that article of creed climbs down from the books and into your kitchen, your doorstep, and your tongue. You speak for the Day you will be asked; you host for the Day you will be asked. Creed drives conduct — that is the whole architecture of the ḥadīth.

ميزان اللسان — فليقل خيرًا أو ليصمت The Tongue Rule — Weigh Before You Speak

The first command hands you a scale. Before a word leaves you, weigh it: if it is خَيْر (good) — truth, ذكر, knowledge, kindness, mending between people — then say it, for good speech is itself worship. If it is not good, or is merely idle chatter with no benefit in it, then لِيَصْمُتْ — let him remain silent — and that silence is not emptiness but an act of worship, chosen for Allah's sake. Ibn Rajab observes in جامع العلوم والحكم that the ḥadīth is explicit: speech divides into خير which the believer says, and everything else, from which he withholds his tongue. There is no honoured third category of "harmless" babble — what is not good is, at best, a waste the believer leaves, and at worst a door to sin.

This makes the command a sister of Ḥadīth 12 — من حسن إسلام المرء تركه ما لا يعنيه, "part of the excellence of a person's Islam is leaving what does not concern him." What does not concern you, you leave; and speech that carries no خير is the first thing that does not concern you. Together the two ḥadīths give the tongue its complete manners — which you drilled practically in the Islam 101 lesson on آداب الكلام. The believer is not the one who never speaks, nor the one who always speaks: he is the one whose tongue waits for the scale.

حق الجار The Neighbour — a Right That Does Not Ask Who He Is

فَلْيُكْرِمْ جَارَهُ — "let him honour his neighbour." الجار (the neighbour) in Islam holds a right simply by living beside you — the Sharīʿah does not first ask whether he is righteous or heedless, relative or stranger, Muslim or not. The scholars note that neighbours differ in degree — the neighbour who is also kin, the Muslim neighbour, and the non-Muslim neighbour — yet the baseline of إكرام (honouring) covers them all: restraining your harm from him, bearing his, greeting him, checking on him, sharing food with him, guarding his home and honour in his absence. Harming the neighbour is not a small sin dressed in ordinary clothes; the Prophet ﷺ tied faith itself to the neighbour's safety from one's harm.

How heavy is this right? The Prophet ﷺ said that جبريل (Jibrīl) kept advising him about the neighbour until he thought the neighbour would be made an heir — that revelation was about to give him a fixed share of your estate like a brother or son. Sit with that image: the greatest angel, descending repeatedly to the greatest Prophet ﷺ, and the subject each time is — the man next door. Ibn Rajab counts honouring the neighbour among the plainest proofs that īmān is not a private feeling but a debt paid outward: your creed has a street address, and your neighbour reads it every day.

إكرام الضيف The Guest — فَلْيُكْرِمْ ضَيْفَهُ

The third command: فَلْيُكْرِمْ ضَيْفَهُ — "let him honour his guest." الضَّيْف (the guest) is the one who alights at your home, and الضيافة (hospitality) is his right upon you, not a favour you may grant or withhold. Honouring him means a warm face at the door, generosity with what you have without strain or showing off, attending to his comfort, and serving him yourself — the way إبراهيم عليه السلام hurried to his guests with a roasted calf before they had even asked. The scholars mention that the fullest hospitality is the first day and night, the guest's right runs to three days, and whatever follows is charity — a scale that keeps generosity generous without crushing the host.

If this clause sounds familiar, it should: the Arabic track's hospitality lesson, الضيافة, is built around this very wording — فَلْيُكْرِمْ ضَيْفَهُ — teaching you the vocabulary of welcoming while this lesson gives you its chain and its fiqh. The two lessons now shake hands across the tabs: there you learned to say welcome; here you learn that the welcome itself is an article of your إيمان acted out. One clause of one ḥadīth, met twice from two directions — that is how memorization becomes understanding.

A gem from Ibn Rajab — فائدة

Three commands, one condition. The Prophet ﷺ could have said the refrain once and listed the three duties after it; instead he re-lit the same lamp before each door — مَنْ كَانَ يُؤْمِنُ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ — so that no one could imagine the tongue is faith's business but the doorstep is not. Ibn Rajab's summary of the ḥadīth is that it gathers the manners of الإيمان in speech and action alike; and the pattern is precise: الإيمان produces الإحسان in your words (speak good or be silent), across your walls (the neighbour), and at your welcome (the guest). One hidden root — belief in Allah and the Last Day — and three visible branches that everyone around you can taste. If you want to measure the root, look at the branches.

Memorize & Renew — احفظ وجدّد

مَنْ كَانَ يُؤْمِنُ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ فَلْيَقُلْ خَيْرًا أَوْ لِيَصْمُتْ

"Whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day, let him speak good or remain silent." Memorize this first clause word-perfect, then add the neighbour and the guest — the refrain repeats, so the whole ḥadīth builds itself. And before your next sentence leaves you, run it across the scale: is it خير? Say it. Is it not? The silence is worship.
