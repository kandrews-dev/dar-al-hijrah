Ḥadīth 14 of 42 · Agreed uponYear 2

حُرْمَةُ دَمِ الْمُسْلِمِ

The Sanctity of Muslim Blood

Objectives — أهداف الدرس

  • Memorize the ḥadīth of ابن مسعود — «لَا يَحِلُّ دَمُ امْرِئٍ مُسْلِمٍ…» — word-perfect.
  • Understand that the default this ḥadīth establishes is the حرمة (sanctity) of Muslim blood — it is a protection text, the twin of Ḥadīth 8 — supported by the āyah ﴿وَمَن يَقْتُلْ مُؤْمِنًا مُّتَعَمِّدًا فَجَزَاؤُهُ جَهَنَّمُ﴾.
  • Learn that the three exceptions are حدود (fixed penalties) and قصاص (just retribution) belonging exclusively to the legitimate judicial authority after due process — never to individuals.
  • Know each of the three exceptions at the overview level: الثيب الزاني, النفس بالنفس, and التارك لدينه المفارق للجماعة.
  • Renew the ʿaqīdah rule from Ḥadīth 8: the shahādah protects, judgments run on the outward, and the inward is Allah's — a shield against hasty takfīr.

عَنِ ابْنِ مَسْعُودٍ رَضِيَ اللهُ عَنْهُ قَالَ: قَالَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ ﷺ: «لَا يَحِلُّ دَمُ امْرِئٍ مُسْلِمٍ يَشْهَدُ أَنْ لَا إِلَهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَأَنِّي رَسُولُ اللَّهِ إِلَّا بِإِحْدَى ثَلَاثٍ: الثَّيِّبُ الزَّانِي، وَالنَّفْسُ بِالنَّفْسِ، وَالتَّارِكُ لِدِينِهِ الْمُفَارِقُ لِلْجَمَاعَةِ» رَوَاهُ الْبُخَارِيُّ وَمُسْلِمٌ.

On the authority of Ibn Masʿūd (may Allah be pleased with him): the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: "The blood of a Muslim who testifies that none has the right to be worshipped but Allah and that I am the Messenger of Allah is not lawful except in one of three cases: the previously-married adulterer, a life for a life, and the one who abandons his religion, separating from the community." Narrated by al-Bukhārī and Muslim.

Narrated by ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd · al-Bukhārī & Muslim · Agreed upon

الراوي عبد الله بن مسعود The Narrator — ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd

عبد الله بن مسعود (ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd) was among the very earliest to accept Islam in Makkah — he counted himself the sixth person to enter the religion — and he was the first to recite the Qur'an aloud publicly at the Kaʿbah. He kept the closest personal attendance on the Prophet ﷺ, carrying his sandals, his سواك, and his water for purification, until the Companions would say his ways so resembled the Prophet's ﷺ own. He became the great faqīh and Qur'an-teacher of Kūfah, and the Prophet ﷺ himself said that whoever wishes to recite the Qur'an fresh as it was revealed should recite it upon the reading of ابن أم عبد — Ibn Masʿūd. A ḥadīth that draws the exact legal boundary around human life — where sanctity is absolute and where, and by whom alone, an exception may ever be applied — is fittingly carried by the most exacting jurist among the Companions.

لَا يَحِلُّ — الأصل حرمة الدم "Is Not Lawful" — the Default Is the Sanctity of Blood

Read the ḥadīth from its headline: لَا يَحِلُّ — "is not lawful." Before it mentions any exception, the ḥadīth lays down a default, and the default is prohibition. Ibn Rajab in جامع العلوم والحكم treats this ḥadīth as one of the great foundations of the religion's protection of life: the blood of every Muslim is حرام (forbidden, sacred) — untouchable — and it remains untouchable unless one of three narrow, legally-defined cases is established before the proper authority. This is a protection text, not a permission text. It is the twin of the ḥadīth you studied at Ḥadīth 8 — عصمة الدم والمال: there the Prophet ﷺ said عَصَمُوا مِنِّي دِمَاءَهُمْ وَأَمْوَالَهُمْ — "they have protected from me their blood and wealth" — stating the sanctity as the Muslim's acquired right; here he ﷺ states the same sanctity from the other side, as a standing prohibition: no one's blood becomes lawful except by what the sharīʿah itself has fixed.

Notice also what the ḥadīth ties the sanctity to: يَشْهَدُ أَنْ لَا إِلَهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَأَنِّي رَسُولُ اللَّهِ — the shahādatān. The testimony itself is the cause of the protection. Shaykh al-ʿUthaymīn notes in his sharḥ that describing the Muslim by his testimony — rather than simply saying "a Muslim" — points to the reason for the ruling: it is the kalimah that transfers a person into the fortress of Islam, and it is the kalimah that makes his blood sacred. This is the same door you entered in معنى لا إله إلا الله — the Meaning of the Kalimah.

The Qur'an guards this same sanctity with one of its most fearsome threats. Allah says: ﴿وَمَن يَقْتُلْ مُؤْمِنًا مُّتَعَمِّدًا فَجَزَاؤُهُ جَهَنَّمُ﴾ (al-Nisāʼ 4:93) — "And whoever kills a believer intentionally — his recompense is Hell." The scholars stand in awe of this āyah: the unlawful taking of one believing life is answered with the threat of جهنم itself. And in the Farewell Sermon the Prophet ﷺ declared the blood, wealth, and honour of the Muslims sacred among them like the sanctity of that day, that month, and that city. The whole sharīʿah leans one way here — towards protection.

إِلَّا بِإِحْدَى ثَلَاثٍ — حكم للقضاء لا للأفراد "Except in One of Three" — Rulings for the Judiciary, Never for Individuals

Before studying the three cases, fix the rule that governs all of them. The exceptions are حدود (fixed penalties) and قصاص (just retribution) — and in the sharīʿah these belong exclusively to the legitimate judicial authority: the imām, the judge, the established court — with proof, testimony, procedure, and due process. Ibn Rajab and Shaykh al-ʿUthaymīn are explicit on this: no individual may ever appoint himself the enforcer of any of the three. The ḥadīth describes whose blood may be judged, not who may judge — and the one who takes blood into his own hands has not applied this ḥadīth but violated it, for he has committed the very crime against sanctity that the ḥadīth exists to forbid.

This is the identical rule you learned at Ḥadīth 8 under إِلَّا بِحَقِّ الْإِسْلَامِ — "except by the right of Islam": the exception clause is a door for the courts of Islam, never a door for individuals. The two ḥadīths teach one law from two sides — the sanctity is everyone's to honour; the exceptions are the authority's alone to apply.

الثَّيِّبُ الزَّانِي The First Exception — the Previously-Married Adulterer

الثَّيِّب (the previously married) is the one who has entered a valid marriage and consummated it. The sharīʿah distinguishes such a person from the unmarried: for the unmarried who falls into زنا (unlawful intercourse) there is a lesser prescribed penalty, while the ثيب who commits it — having tasted the lawful and then trampled the sanctity of marriage — is liable to the capital ḥadd. But note how the sharīʿah then fences this penalty with an evidentiary bar that is nearly impossible to meet: establishing زنا requires four upright eyewitnesses to the act itself, explicit and unmistakable — or a free, unforced confession before the judge. The scholars draw the lesson from this: the sharīʿah's aim in this ḥadd is deterrence and the public honouring of marriage's sanctity, not the pursuit of people — and, like every ḥadd, it is established and carried out only by the court, never by any individual, family, or crowd.

وَالنَّفْسُ بِالنَّفْسِ — القصاص The Second Exception — a Life for a Life (Qiṣāṣ)

النَّفْسُ بِالنَّفْسِ — "a life for a life" — is القصاص (just retribution): the one who intentionally and unlawfully kills a protected soul is liable, by court judgment, to be executed for it. The scholars highlight the exact justice in the wording — a life for a life: the penalty answers the crime measure for measure, nothing beyond it. And the sharīʿah opens a door of mercy inside the justice: the right of qiṣāṣ belongs to أولياء الدم (the victim's heirs), and they may choose to pardon the killer entirely, or to accept the دية (blood-payment) in place of retribution — and pardon is beloved to Allah. All of it — the proof, the judgment, the execution or the settlement — runs through the judge; the family's right is a right they claim before the court, never a licence for revenge by their own hands.

وَالتَّارِكُ لِدِينِهِ الْمُفَارِقُ لِلْجَمَاعَةِ The Third Exception — the One Who Abandons His Religion, Separating from the Community

The third case joins two descriptions: التَّارِكُ لِدِينِهِ (the one who abandons his religion) الْمُفَارِقُ لِلْجَمَاعَةِ (separating from the community) — apostasy from Islam joined with cutting oneself off from the jamāʿah of the Muslims. The shurrāḥ note that the second description explains and confirms the first: by leaving the religion the person has torn himself out of the very community whose testimony had made his blood sacred. And here more than anywhere the rule of authority holds: this is a matter of the imām and the state alone. The scholars note that the authority first practises الاستتابة (seeking the person's repentance) — the door of return is opened, doubts are removed, and the matter is examined by the people of knowledge — before any ruling is carried out. No individual has any share whatsoever in judging or enforcing this case; a person who presumes to do so has committed unlawful killing, the very crime of 4:93.

الشهادة عاصمة — الظاهر لنا والسرائر لله The Shahādah Protects — the Outward Is Ours, the Inward Is Allah's

Return to the ḥadīth's description of the protected person: he testifies. The ḥadīth runs on the outward profession — exactly as Ḥadīth 8 closed with وَحِسَابُهُمْ عَلَى اللَّهِ: we deal with people by their ظاهر (outward), and the reckoning of the باطن (inward) belongs to Allah alone. Whoever pronounces the shahādatān is treated as a Muslim, and his blood carries the full sanctity of this ḥadīth — suspicion, sin, and hasty judgment do not strip it away. This is the standing shield against the ancient error of the Khawārij: declaring Muslims outside the fold and treating their blood as lawful. The nullifiers of Islam are real — but their application to any specific person is the work of the scholars and judges alone, with conditions and impediments carefully weighed. Revisit نواقض الإسلام — the Nullifiers of Islam and its warnings against hasty takfīr: this ḥadīth is one of its strongest proof-texts, for it hangs the sanctity of blood on the testimony itself and then locks every exception behind the door of the court.

A gem from Ibn Rajab — فائدة

Ibn Rajab draws out the thread that binds the three exceptions into one: in every case, the offender first breached a protected sanctity belonging to another. The ثيب who commits زنا tramples the sanctity of marriage and lineage; the killer destroys the sanctity of a life; the apostate who splits from the jamāʿah tears the sanctity of the community itself. So the ḥadīth never makes blood cheap — it teaches the opposite: blood becomes liable only when its owner has first torn down a حرمة (sanctity) the sharīʿah guards. The law of Islam does not begin with punishment; it begins with protection, and its penalties exist only to defend the very sanctities this ḥadīth proclaims.
