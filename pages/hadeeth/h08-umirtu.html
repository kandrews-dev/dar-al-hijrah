Ḥadīth 8 of 42 · Agreed upon

عِصْمَةُ الدَّمِ وَالمَال

The Sanctity of Muslim Blood and Wealth

Objectives — أهداف الدرس

  • Memorize the ḥadīth of ابن عمر — «أُمِرْتُ أَنْ أُقَاتِلَ النَّاسَ…» — word-perfect.
  • Understand the passive أُمِرْتُ: the command is from Allah — the Prophet ﷺ does not act from himself.
  • Learn to whom the ruling of قتال (fighting) belongs — the imām and legitimate authority, not individuals — and how the scholars harmonise «النَّاسَ» with ﴿لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ﴾ and the texts of covenant and jizyah.
  • Grasp العصمة — the sanctity of Muslim blood and wealth once the shahādatān, prayer, and zakāh are established — and the exception إِلَّا بِحَقِّ الْإِسْلَامِ.
  • Learn the ʿaqīdah rule of وَحِسَابُهُمْ عَلَى اللَّهِ: we judge by the outward, and the inward reckoning belongs to Allah alone — a shield against hasty takfīr.

عَنِ ابْنِ عُمَرَ رَضِيَ اللهُ عَنْهُمَا أَنَّ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ ﷺ قَالَ: «أُمِرْتُ أَنْ أُقَاتِلَ النَّاسَ حَتَّى يَشْهَدُوا أَنْ لَا إِلَهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَأَنَّ مُحَمَّدًا رَسُولُ اللَّهِ، وَيُقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ، وَيُؤْتُوا الزَّكَاةَ، فَإِذَا فَعَلُوا ذَلِكَ عَصَمُوا مِنِّي دِمَاءَهُمْ وَأَمْوَالَهُمْ إِلَّا بِحَقِّ الْإِسْلَامِ، وَحِسَابُهُمْ عَلَى اللَّهِ تَعَالَى» رَوَاهُ الْبُخَارِيُّ وَمُسْلِمٌ.

On the authority of Ibn ʿUmar (may Allah be pleased with them both): the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: "I have been commanded to fight the people until they testify that none has the right to be worshipped but Allah and that Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah, and establish the prayer, and give the zakāh; if they do that, their blood and wealth are protected from me — except by the right of Islam — and their reckoning is with Allah, Most High." Narrated by al-Bukhārī and Muslim.

Narrated by Ibn ʿUmar · al-Bukhārī & Muslim

الراوي عبد الله بن عمر The Narrator — ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar

عبد الله بن عمر (ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar), son of عمر بن الخطاب, accepted Islam as a boy in Makkah and made hijrah with his father. He is one of the العبادلة — the four famous ʿAbdullāhs among the young Companions — and one of the most prolific narrators of ḥadīth in the Ummah. What the scholars single out about him is his intense الاتباع (following of the Sunnah): he would trace the Prophet's ﷺ footsteps, pray where he prayed, and stop where he stopped, fearful of adding or omitting anything. A ḥadīth that draws the exact boundary of a ruling — where fighting ends, where sanctity begins, and where judgment passes to Allah — is fittingly carried by a narrator known above all for exactness.

أُمِرْتُ — الآمر هو الله "I Have Been Commanded" — the Commander Is Allah

The ḥadīth opens with a passive verb: أُمِرْتُ — "I have been commanded." The scholars pause here, because the doer is deliberately unnamed and needs no naming: when the Prophet ﷺ says "I was commanded," the Commander can only be Allah. Shaykh al-ʿUthaymīn notes in his sharḥ that this single word settles the nature of everything that follows — the Prophet ﷺ does not act from himself, does not legislate from himself, and does not fight from himself. He is عبد مأمور (a commanded servant), conveying and executing what his Lord revealed: ﴿وَمَا يَنْطِقُ عَنِ الْهَوَى ۝ إِنْ هُوَ إِلَّا وَحْيٌ يُوحَى﴾ (al-Najm 53:3–4) — "Nor does he speak from desire; it is only revelation revealed." So this ruling is not a policy the Prophet ﷺ devised; it is دين (religion) from Allah, with the limits Allah set for it — and those limits are exactly what the rest of the ḥadīth and the rest of the texts lay out.

من المخاطب بالقتال؟ — حكم للإمام لا للأفراد Whom Does the Ḥadīth Address? A Ruling for the Imām, Not for Individuals

The shurrāḥ (commentators) are explicit about what this ḥadīth is and is not. It speaks of قتال (fighting, combat) — the collective, state-level matter of jihad — and that matter belongs in the sharīʿah to the imām and the legitimate authority: declaring it, leading it, and binding it by treaties and covenants. It is not an address to individuals, and no person may take it as a licence for his own conduct. The scholars also note the wording itself: the Prophet ﷺ said أُقَاتِلَ ("that I fight," from مُقَاتَلَة — mutual, declared combat between collectives), not أَقْتُلَ ("that I kill") — fighting a hostile, defiant collective under authority is a different ruling entirely from any individual act, and the two are never interchangeable.

As for «النَّاسَ» ("the people") — Ibn Rajab in جامع العلوم والحكم and the shurrāḥ after him teach that it is عام مخصوص: a general wording understood through the other texts that qualify it, for the texts of the sharīʿah explain one another and are never read in isolation. Allah says: ﴿لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ﴾ (al-Baqarah 2:256) — "There is no compulsion in the religion" — and the sharīʿah itself affirms أهل العهد (the people of covenant) and أهل الجزية (the people of jizyah): non-Muslims who live under treaty or under the protection of the Muslim state, whom it is forbidden to fight or harm. So the ḥadīth cannot mean every human being without exception; the people of covenant and jizyah are excluded by other explicit texts, and Ibn Rajab addresses exactly this harmonisation. The ḥadīth describes the collective struggle against hostile polytheism in its time and place, under the Prophet's ﷺ own command — with its end-point built into its wording: the fighting stops at the shahādah. Its purpose is not the destruction of people but the opening of the door of Islam and the protection of those who enter it.

The shurrāḥ also compare the parallel narrations: in the wording of Abū Hurayrah the fighting ends at the statement of the shahādah alone, while Ibn ʿUmar's wording adds establishing the prayer and giving the zakāh. Ibn Rajab explains that the narrations complete one another — entry into Islam is by the shahādatān, and the prayer and zakāh are the greatest outward marks by which that entry is confirmed and by which Islam's protection is fully settled, as the next clause states.

عَصَمُوا مِنِّي دِمَاءَهُمْ وَأَمْوَالَهُمْ — العصمة "Their Blood and Wealth Are Protected" — the Sanctity of the Muslim

Here is the fiqh payoff of the ḥadīth — the ruling the scholars quote it for. عَصَمُوا is from العِصْمَة (sanctity, inviolability): the moment a person testifies the shahādatān and establishes the prayer and gives the zakāh, his دِمَاء (blood) and his أَمْوَال (wealth) become inviolable — untouchable, legally protected. Notice the direction of the ḥadīth: it is not a threat hanging over people; it is a guarantee given to them. The Prophet ﷺ states it as their acquired right: عَصَمُوا مِنِّي — "they have protected from me their blood and wealth." Once the outward marks of Islam are present, no one — not even the Messenger of Allah ﷺ himself, by his own words — may touch a Muslim's life or property.

This is the very foundation you met in the Islam 101 track: the shahādatān as the door of the religion, and the prayer and zakāh as the first pillars built upon it — see أركان الإسلام الخمسة — the Five Pillars and the unit on معنى لا إله إلا الله — the Meaning of the Kalimah. This ḥadīth is the proof-text behind both: the kalimah is not a mere phrase — it is the word that transfers a person from one legal state to another, granting his life and property the full sanctity of Islam.

إِلَّا بِحَقِّ الْإِسْلَامِ — الاستثناء "Except by the Right of Islam" — the Exception

The sanctity is complete, but not unconditional in an absolute sense: إِلَّا بِحَقِّ الْإِسْلَامِ — "except by the right of Islam." Islam itself, within its own law, attaches rights to blood and wealth: القصاص (just retribution) for one who takes a life, the prescribed penalties for crimes the sharīʿah has fixed, and the zakāh as a right owed in wealth. Shaykh al-ʿUthaymīn stresses what this exception does and does not mean: these rights are claimed only through the sharīʿah's own justice system — the imām, the judge, the established authority — with proof, procedure, and due process. The exception clause is a door for the courts of Islam, never a door for individuals. No person may appoint himself the claimant of "the right of Islam" against another's blood or property; whoever does so has violated the very sanctity this ḥadīth establishes.

وَحِسَابُهُمْ عَلَى اللَّهِ — الظاهر لنا والسرائر لله "Their Reckoning Is with Allah" — the Outward Is Ours, the Inward Is Allah's

The ḥadīth closes with a rule of ʿaqīdah: وَحِسَابُهُمْ عَلَى اللَّهِ تَعَالَى — "and their reckoning is with Allah, Most High." Ibn Rajab explains: we are commanded to deal with people by their ظاهر (outward): whoever professes the shahādatān and shows the marks of Islam is treated as a Muslim — his blood sacred, his wealth sacred, prayed with, married among, buried with the Muslims. As for the باطن (inward) — whether the heart is truthful or concealing — that حِسَاب (reckoning) belongs to Allah alone, who alone sees the hearts. This is why the Prophet ﷺ accepted the outward Islam even of those whose hypocrisy was suspected, and it is the famous lesson of أسامة بن زيد, who killed a man in battle after the man said لا إله إلا الله, assuming he said it only to save himself. The Prophet ﷺ rebuked him: «أَقَتَلْتَهُ بَعْدَ مَا قَالَ لَا إِلَهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ؟» — "Did you kill him after he said lā ilāha illā Allāh?" — and kept repeating it until Usāmah wished he had not become Muslim before that day.

From this the scholars derive a standing warning: whoever professes Islam is not expelled from it by suspicion, sin, or hasty judgment. Declaring a Muslim an unbeliever — التكفير — has strict conditions and impediments that only the people of knowledge apply; rushing into it is the ancient error of the Khawārij. Revisit the warnings in the Aqeedah lesson نواقض الإسلام — the Nullifiers of Islam: the nullifiers are real, but their application to a specific person is the work of the scholars and judges, not of zealous individuals — for the outward is ours to honour, and the inward is Allah's to judge.

A gem from Ibn Rajab — فائدة

Ibn Rajab records how أبو بكر الصديق applied this very ḥadīth. When some tribes after the Prophet's ﷺ death professed the shahādah and prayed but refused the zakāh, عمر questioned fighting them, citing the ḥadīth's protection. Abū Bakr answered from the ḥadīth itself: the zakāh is حق المال — a right of Islam in wealth — falling squarely under إِلَّا بِحَقِّ الْإِسْلَامِ, and the ḥadīth had made the protection conditional on establishing prayer and giving zakāh together. The Companions united upon his understanding. So the first generation read this ḥadīth exactly as its clauses sit: protection by its conditions, exception by its right — and it was the khalīfah, the legitimate authority, who carried the ruling out, not individuals.
